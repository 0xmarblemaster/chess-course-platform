At what age should a child start chess?

5 min read · Chingis Nurgalimov, founder

This is the question parents ask us most. The short answer: most children are ready for regular classes at 5–6. At that age a child can sit at the board for 20–30 minutes, remembers the rules and enjoys playing by them.

You can start earlier, from 4, but through play: stories about the pieces, simple tasks on a checkered board, short games on part of the board. The key is not to rush and not to turn it into a lesson your child has to sit through.

How do you know your child is ready? They can tell left from right, know numbers and letters at least up to ten, can wait for their turn in a board game and don't burst into tears after every loss.

If some of this doesn't work yet, that's no reason to wait. The first lesson is exactly where the coach sees your child in action and suggests which group to start in.

At Chess Empire groups have up to 10 students and classes run twice a week — enough for skills to stick while keeping the interest alive. Come to a trial lesson together, see how it works, and decide afterwards.

How chess affects schoolwork and focus

6 min read · Nail Ildusovich, coach

A chess game is a stretch of time in which a child keeps the position in mind, calculates options and owns every decision. For a young student it trains exactly the skill they need in class: staying focused on one task.

The link between chess and schoolwork has been studied for a long time, and researchers are careful in their conclusions: the benefit shows most where classes are regular and led by a coach, not occasional. Chess is no miracle, but it builds the habit of thinking before you move.

After about three months, the parents of our students tend to notice the same things: their child sits over homework longer, gives up on a problem halfway less often and takes mistakes more calmly — in chess a mistake is analysed, not scolded.

The structure of the lesson helps too: a short bit of theory, then puzzles, then a game with a review. The child learns to plan — "if I go here, how will my opponent respond?" — and carries that question into other things.

For it to work, regularity matters: at least two classes a week and a little practice at home. In our experience one lesson a week is too little for the skill to stick.

First tournament: how to prepare your child

4 min read · Assylkhan Agbaevich, FIDE Master

A first tournament is a big event for the child and the parents alike. At Chess Empire children join tournaments after the first stage of training and a test with their coach, so by the start they know the rules well.

The day before, the goal is to calm down, not to cram. Sleep well, have breakfast, pack water and a snack for between rounds. Explain in advance what will happen: several games, a chess clock, a handshake before and after each game.

During the tournament parents are better off staying in the background: hints are against the rules, and a look over the shoulder only adds to the nerves. Between rounds, don't ask "did you win?" — ask "which game was the most interesting?"

Losses will happen — to everyone. Help your child through the first one: listen, don't go over the mistakes right away, let them cool down. Leave the review to the coach — at the next class they will show where the turning point was and turn the loss into a clear lesson.

Tournaments run at our branches every weekend, and the membership includes up to four free tournaments a month. The more often a child plays, the sooner a tournament becomes a fun event rather than an exam.

FIDE rating: what it is and why your child needs it

5 min read · Vasily Mikhaylovich, FIDE Master

A FIDE rating is a number that shows a player's strength under the system of the International Chess Federation. It goes up after wins against stronger opponents and down after losses to weaker ones.

To get a rating, a player needs a certain number of games in tournaments officially rated by FIDE, and some of the opponents must already have a rating. The rules change from time to time, so the coach will tell you the current requirements.

Why does a child need it? A rating is a clear goal and an honest measure of progress. It opens the way to international tournaments and lets a child compare themselves not just with classmates but with players around the world.

There is no need to rush, though. First a child plays club tournaments and city events, gains experience and learns to take results calmly. Rated tournaments are the next step, once the coach sees the student is ready.

At Chess Empire the student path is built in stages — from the first rules to an international FIDE rating. At each stage the coach explains what is left to learn, so parents always know where their child stands.

How to practise chess at home

7 min read · Aleksandr Olegovich, FIDE Master

Many parents worry: "I don't play — how can I help?" The good news: you don't need to be a chess player to support practice at home. Your job is to help your child go over what they learned, a little at a time and with pleasure.

Short sessions work best: 15 minutes three or four times a week does more than an hour on Sunday. Keep the board somewhere visible — it's easier to come back to.

Ask your child to teach you what they learned in class. Explaining how the knight moves or what check is helps them remember it, and gives you a reason to spend time together.

Use the coach's homework: mate-in-one puzzles, games on part of the board, for example with pawns only. If something doesn't work, don't give the answer straight away — ask: "What moves do you have?"

And don't turn practice into a check-up. Praise effort and interesting ideas, not just the right answer. If your child is tired, finish on a good note and come back to the board tomorrow.

Chess instead of gadgets: how to get your child hooked

5 min read · Chingis Nurgalimov, founder

Taking away the tablet and putting a board in its place is not the best plan. Chess beats gadgets not through bans but by giving a child real company, excitement and the feeling of getting stronger.

Start by playing together. Children value an adult's attention: a game with dad or an older brother means more than a cartoon. Let the rules be simplified at first — what matters is that it's fun.

Small goals work well: solve three puzzles in an evening, beat grandma, move up a stage at the club. The child sees their own progress, and the screen stops being the only source of fun.

Friends their own age matter too. In a group a child finds friends with the same hobby, and weekend tournaments become an event they look forward to all week.

If your child still reaches for a screen, you can use that as well: chess puzzles in an app, in moderation, are a normal part of learning. Agree on a rule: first a game on a real board, then the screen.
